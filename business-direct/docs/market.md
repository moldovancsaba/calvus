# business.direct — the market: who the classified media owners are, what they run, what they buy, what nobody sells them

*For the stakeholders. What this document holds: the product's own market — the segment, how it
earns, the software owners run, the tools they buy for the pieces, the gap the product fills, the
product's SWOT. Every figure is a list price or a vendor's own
statement read on 2026-09-20 and marked so; the first customer's category evidence is in the
research rounds indexed in `evidence.md`. Questions: how large is the classified-media segment and how
does it earn; what software do owners run their sites on and what does it not do; what do they
buy today for the pieces of sales and marketing, at what price; where is the gap business.direct
fills; and the product's own SWOT. **P** primary source opened, **A** secondary; market-size
figures are vendor research and conflict with each other — they are given as ranges and never
used as a claim in the top layer. The first customer's category evidence stays in research I–III.*

## 1. The segment — classified media owners

- **Size and growth (A, conflicting).** Vendor forecasts put the online classified ad platform
  market between **$33.9 B (2026, 6.2 % CAGR to 2034)** and **$43.8 B (2026, 13.4 % CAGR)**;
  vertical classifieds (real estate, automotive, jobs, services) ≈ 57 % of revenue;
  advertisement-based models ≈ 62 % of revenue. **A** [Intel Market Research](https://www.intelmarketresearch.com/online-classified-ad-platform-market-36029),
  [Technavio](https://www.technavio.com/report/online-classified-ad-platform-market-industry-analysis),
  [Persistence](https://www.persistencemarketresearch.com/market-research/classified-platform-market.asp),
  [Statista outlook](https://www.statista.com/outlook/dmo/digital-advertising/classifieds/worldwide).
  The number that matters for the product is not the market's size but its **shape**: thousands
  of vertical and local sites, most run by one to five people.
- **How they earn.** Pay-per-listing and subscription fees, premium placement (featured, top of
  category, badges), performance fees tied to leads, banner and sponsored content; the
  directory-software vendors' own guidance shows the typical tiers — **free · $29 · $79 a month**
  — and that "telemarketing has the highest conversion for selling premium listings; one yes in
  ten calls is a good start", and that "showing businesses views, clicks and leads makes them
  renew and upgrade". **A** [eDirectory: how directory websites make money](https://www.edirectory.com/updates/how-directory-websites-make-money/),
  [eDirectory: how to monetize](https://www.edirectory.com/newsletter/how-to-monetize-your-directory.php),
  [Directorist](https://directorist.com/blog/how-to-monetize-a-directory-website/),
  [GeoDirectory: six rules](https://wpgeodirectory.com/the-6-rules-to-monetize-a-directory-website/).
  A small niche directory earns **$100–500 a month**; an established one **$5 000–50 000+** from
  premium listings, ads and lead fees. **A** [eDirectory](https://www.edirectory.com/updates/how-directory-websites-make-money/)
- **The three jobs, in the vendors' own words.** Sell the listings (premium tiers, by phone and
  e-mail), fill the site (SEO, content, social — every guide's second chapter), keep the
  advertisers (analytics that show results at renewal). The vendors describe the jobs; they do
  not do them.

Stacked, an owner pays **$200–400 a month** for the pieces and remains the person who runs them
(research II §4; the anchors above). A managing advertiser on the site would pay another
$99–400 for its own answering and review tools — which the product gives it inside the site.

## 4. The gap business.direct fills

Nobody sells the classified media owner **the team**: supply-side sales that works every listing
through claim → placement, demand-side content generated from the listings and delivered to
every medium, retention that watches the advertisers, and the owner's economics as a screen —
operated as departments with a human gate and a policy gate, on the site's own data, with each
managing advertiser getting its own team inside the site. The directory software vendors say
what the owner should do; the agent platforms sell generic agents; the local-business platforms
sell to the advertiser, not the owner. The product sits between them and is bought by the
one who owns the inventory.

## 5. The product's SWOT (the first instance's pilot SWOT is `logic-audit.md` §3)

| | Evidenced |
|---|---|
| **Strengths** | S1 the buyer owns the inventory and the data — one contract covers every listing; S2 the human gate and the policy gate are the operating rule, not a feature (research IV, the enforcement record); S3 the economics screen is the argument — the product proves its own ROI to the owner from the first cohort; S4 the second site is a policy record and a connector (ADR-2); S5 a managing advertiser gets what Podium and Yelp sell it, inside the owner's site — the reason it claims, stays and upgrades |
| **Weaknesses** | W1 no customer beyond the first; the product's own funnel and CAC are unmeasured; W2 the product depends on each site's API and data quality (reviews, prices, contacts) it does not control (`logic-audit.md` A15, A16); W3 the operator is the bottleneck by design until departments earn auto-approval (`logic-audit.md` A10); W4 the vendor stack is behind interfaces but the clip engine and Meta publishing carry external review and rate limits (research VI) |
| **Opportunities** | O1 a fragmented segment of thousands of vertical and local sites run by one to five people, sold software that describes the jobs; O2 incumbents validate the willingness to pay for answering and lead management ($99–400 a month per business — K12, §3) — the owner-level product aggregates it; O3 AI answers and social search reward the structured, published source (K7–K9): the content job has a measurable payoff; O4 outcome-priced agents (Breeze) set an expectation the product can meet with measured events |
| **Threats** | T1 the directory-software vendors add "AI marketing" to the site product; T2 HubSpot / Salesforce agents grow a marketplace vocabulary; T3 platform rules and law move under the channels (labels, consent, children's data — research IV, V); T4 deliverability and channel review are single points of failure for two of the three jobs |

**What the SWOT says to do:** measure the product's own funnel from the first customer (W1);
make the policy gate and the economics screen the demo (S2, S3); the stack the owner replaces and
the per-business tools the advertisers would otherwise buy (O2) are the comparison the owner will
make; keep every channel behind an adapter and every vendor behind an interface (T4, W4).

## 6. What was not found

- No primary data on advertiser churn or renewal rates for classified sites; the vendors' guides
  say "retention is cheaper than acquisition" without numbers. The product measures it.
- No independent survey of classified media owners' spend on marketing and sales tools; the
  $200–400 stack is a sum of list prices, not a measured basket.
- Market-size figures differ by 30 % between research vendors and are not used as claims.
