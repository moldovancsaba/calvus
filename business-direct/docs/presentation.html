1 · The customer — the one-person army

A marketplace founder with thousands of listings and nobody to sell to the businesses on them or publish to the people who use them. A studio owner. A consultant. An agency of one. Any business where one person is sales and marketing — and something else all day.

The week they have

Seven jobs, one calendar

Research what the market is doing. Write and publish content on every channel. Cut the video. Find prospects, contact them, follow up three times. Answer every enquiry the same hour. Ask for reviews and answer them. Read the numbers and decide where the next dollar goes.

The week they get

Answering wins; everything else waits

Content goes out when there is time — there is no time. Prospects get one message and no follow-up. Enquiries wait until evening. Numbers are a feeling. The tools they buy — a scheduler, a clip cutter, a CRM, a chatbot — each does one job and leaves the person to be the team.

What they want

A team they can trust, that they still control

Someone who does the work in their voice, from their own material, follows their rules, never sends anything they have not seen, and tells them on Monday what happened and what to do next.

2 · The complication — what doing it alone costs

Enquiries die in the inbox

Response time is the business: 21× more qualification within five minutes; the odds of reaching a lead at all fall 100× between minute five and minute thirty; 62 % of calls to small businesses are never answered; 78 % of customers buy from the first responder.

MIT / InsideSales; 411 Locals; research I §2

Content that is not published does not exist

Organic Google sends 19 % fewer clicks to the same rankings; nearly half of Gen Z searches social before Google; AI answers cite the structured, reviewed source. The operator's own material — sessions, offers, news, footage — sits unpublished.

research II §1, §3

Follow-up is where the money is, and it does not happen

Reminders cut no-shows by 29 %; a three-touch sequence in the right order is the difference between a 3 % and a 10 % reply rate. One person sends the first message and forgets the second.

The market sells pieces, not a team

Yelp bought Hatch for $270 M and sells "Receptionist" at $99 a month — one job. Schedulers at $29–200 a month — one job. Clip cutters, avatar makers, chat widgets — one job each. Stacked, they cost $200–400 a month and still leave the person running the department.

3 · The question

How does one person run a seven-department sales and marketing team every day — without hiring, without losing control of what goes out under their name, and without breaking the law or their customers' trust?

4 · The product — business.direct

Departments as automations. Your knowledge as plain files. Two flows at the front door. A human gate. One loop.

Marketing — bring the audience to you

Content from what you already have

The machine reads your offers, dates, news and footage and drafts the week's posts, the digest and the clips — in your voice, from your files. You approve; it publishes at the slot, answers the comments with a drafted reply, and reports what each post brought.

Sales — work every prospect

A pipeline that runs itself, lawfully

Every prospect is scored and worked through identified → contacted → replied → applied → managing → upgraded: three touches in propensity order from a warmed domain, the legal footer on every message, opt-outs honoured, a call list for the phone-only, a drafted answer to every reply, and the upgrade offered only when the machine has delivered more than it costs.

Conversations — answer in the hour

Every enquiry, every channel

A message, an e-mail, a missed call, a comment, a DM: drafted from your files within a minute, approved with one tap, sent on the same channel, the reply time logged. "Not now" pauses everything for that person and hands you a human task.

The human gate

It drafts. It cannot send. It stops and asks.

Nothing reaches a customer, a prospect or a channel without an approval. Simple mode shows what needs you as ranked recommendations with the reason and one button; Advanced shows every screen and input. A department that runs four clean weeks earns auto-approval for routine messages — every one logged and stoppable.

The loop

Events → metrics → the next dollar → you → measure

Every touch, reply, post, save, ask and payment is an event. Nightly: cost per customer won, lifetime value, payback, the value of an engaged audience member, cost per audience member from content. Weekly: three levers ranked by expected value per dollar — another touch, the call list, a week of content. Monday: the recap says what moved and what to decide.

The hands

Integrations, and your own files

E-mail, Instagram and Facebook, push, SMS with consent, the phone, Stripe for what you sell, your own platform's API. Your voice, rules, offers and answers live in plain files the machine reads before every draft — edit a file, and every department changes.

5 · The value — what changes in a week, and what it costs

What the operator gets back

  • The week: seven departments' output for about twenty minutes of approvals a day — the routine is on the Home screen.
  • Every enquiry answered within the hour instead of 62 % lost — the single largest lever in a small business's revenue.
  • Every prospect touched three times in order, and every reply answered — a pipeline instead of a list.
  • Every week's content published from material that already exists, with the numbers behind each post.
  • A Monday recap that names the next dollar, with the assumption it rests on and when it becomes measured.

What it costs — the pricing hypothesis

business.direct is a subscription per operator instance. Anchors: the tools it replaces stack to $200–400 a month for one person doing the work; Yelp's one-job Receptionist is $99. Hypothesis: $249 a month for the machine (all departments, the human gate, the recap), $449 with the AI departments, the clip engine and generated media, an instance fee for a second brand. Running an instance costs the product $50–75 a month at pilot volume (every vendor's price read on the day). The price is the owner's decision, marked as a hypothesis in the business case until set.

The business case

The honest part: the machine reports its own economics to the operator — including when a channel does not pay. For the first customer it already found that outbound e-mail alone, on their catalogue at their sample prices, returns 0.7 of its cost — and showed the three inputs that change it. A team that tells you the truth about the next dollar is the product.

7 · Responsible by design — for every customer

What the customer gets

  • Lower exposure: the enforcement record in consumer marketing is a list of wrong defaults and missing labels (Epic $520 M, YouTube $170 M, Disney $10 M); the product's policy gate makes the wrong default impossible to ship.
  • A better audience: every message goes to a person who saved, asked or opted in — volunteered data converts better than inferred data and needs no profiling.
  • Trust as the channel: half of privacy-aware consumers have switched over data practices; 83 % of small businesses grow on referrals.
  • An audit trail: every send carries its legal basis, its consent and its cap.

How it is done

  • One policy record per customer — jurisdictions and laws, audience model, consent per channel, cap, retention, disclosure — and a gate in the code that blocks a feature until its fields are set.
  • A child is an age, never a name; no profiling or targeting of minors, ever; real footage of children only with written parental consent.
  • No dark patterns, no false urgency, no audience on a protected characteristic; "not now" pauses marketing and gets a human; disclosure when a machine wrote or made something.

8 · Delivery — the product build

Build

Nine two-week sprints, seventeen weeks, two developers; an acceptance test at every sprint review. Sales and conversations first (sprints 2–3), social publishing when the channel's review clears (submitted in sprint 0), audiences and the digest by sprint 6, campaigns and payments by sprint 8, the second-instance proof in sprint 9.

Run cost

≈ $50–75 a month per instance at pilot volume, ≈ $280–400 at 1 000 prospects and 50 000 audience members — every vendor's own price, read on the day, behind an interface so any vendor can be swapped.

Stack

Next.js on Vercel, MongoDB Atlas, Redis, e-mail, Meta, Twilio, Stripe, the Claude API behind a drafter interface, one small worker for video, C2PA credentials on every asset. Seventeen modules specified with pseudo code.

What the first customer provides — after the decision

Nothing today. Then: a postal address for the legal footer, one paragraph in their privacy policy (we draft it), their policy record confirmed, counsel's wording; later their analytics events, a write key and a pilot prospect.
