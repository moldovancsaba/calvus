How the machine decides — the data-driven flow

Every message the machine sends and every dollar it spends comes out of one loop. It is what the Economics screen shows and what the analytics document specifies; the industry's cases behind it are on the Research III page.

1 · Events

Every touch, reply, stage change, approval, post, click, save, ask, booking and payment is a row in an append-only log — the platform's own data, never a third-party analytics store.

2 · Metrics, nightly

CAC per managing and per paying provider; LTV, payback and the 3 : 1 line; the value of an avid family; cost per family from content. Each rate is labelled measured, benchmark or assumption — and flips to measured at 100 observations.

3 · The next dollar

Three levers ranked every week by expected lifetime value gained per dollar: one more e-mail touch, the call list, a week of the content engine. The recap names the winner.

4 · A person approves

The ranking is a recommendation. The operator approves the sequence, the provider approves the campaign, the family sets her preferences — the human gate does not move. It does get lighter: a week's real-footage clips are one decision, and a department that runs four clean weeks earns auto-approval for its routine messages, every one still logged and stoppable.

5 · Measure, then adjust the rules

The sequence cadence, the content slots per neighbourhood and the moment the upgrade card appears follow the measured numbers (rules R17–R19); the first causal test — a neighbourhood holdout, Upper West Side against Upper East Side — is running on the Economics screen, and cohorts by channel stay marked assumption until 100 observations.

What it already found

At the defaults, outbound e-mail to a 252-provider catalogue does not pay for $49 upgrades (LTV : CAC 0.7). The model says the base machine is the platform's growth engine, or the catalogue grows, or the price rises — each one input away.

Responsible by design — for every client

The machine uses customers' data commercially and properly, and it respects children's rights whatever the client. One policy record per instance and a gate in the code — not a policy document.

A child is an age, never a name

No child's name, photo or location exists in the machine — on any instance. Your Field's own policy says it collects no children's data; the machine matches it and holds the line for the next client too.

No profiling of minors, ever

Not with consent, not with a parent's consent — the floor the EU's Digital Services Act, California, Oregon and Nebraska set. Audiences come from what people saved, asked and opted in to.

The gate is code

Provider e-mail needs the postal address; the digest needs the privacy policy to describe it; SMS needs the consent text; generated media needs the disclosure rule. A missing field blocks the feature and says why — the Policy screen is the checklist.

What the client gets

Lower exposure (the enforcement record is a list of defaults and labels: Epic $520 M, YouTube $170 M, Disney $10 M); a better-converting audience (volunteered data beats inferred); trust as the acquisition channel in a category where half of privacy-aware consumers have switched over data practices; and an audit trail on every send.
